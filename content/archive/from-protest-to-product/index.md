**The Hovercraft archive · March 1999**

*Popular Music, Culture and Ideology*, by Aaron Downing (Ron Nasty).

![Title page of From Protest To Product? by Aaron Downing, March 1999](/archive/from-protest-to-product/title-page.jpg)

Three years after Hovercraft fell apart, Ron had some questions about popular music.

Who owns it? Who decides what it means? What happens when music born of protest becomes something to buy and sell?

In March 1999 he submitted *From Protest To Product?* as his independent study for a BA (Hons) in Applied Social Science at the University of Lincolnshire and Humberside. It sets out to test a hypothesis: that *"there is a clear and identifiable link between music, culture and ideology."* It follows that question from the blues, through mass culture, consumer society and youth culture, to punk rock and the Sex Pistols.

Twenty-seven years later, with Hovercraft back in circulation, the questions haven't gone away.

### From the text

On punk:

> Punk stood against the perceived divisiveness and racism of the British class system and its establishment, looking towards a value of acceptance of difference and allowance for individual expression. *(p35)*

On what happened next:

> However, its protest was soon packaged, produced and sold to an increasingly media hyped public. The Sex Pistols interview E.P. *Some Product* and the album released after their demise *Flogging A Dead Horse* encapsulating this notion. *(p38)*

On his own study, with the kind of honesty that rarely survives into print:

> In conclusion of this study and firstly in retrospect there are many areas that have been missed or are cloudy in reasoning, due in many respects to my own lack of specialised, specific knowledge. *(p39)*

And the passage that matters most, written three years after the band split:

> It moves you emotionally, touches your soul, encourages thought, makes you want to dance, or sit back and relax. It has an energy that can pull people together from different cultural, socio-economic and ethnic backgrounds, as it gives people commonality; (maybe this is the power of standardisation?) *(p42)*

He ends with a footnote, written at the tail end of Britpop:

> As a cursory note I always find it amusing in attempting to identify old Teds, Mods, Rockers, Skins and Punks in public places. They are still all out there and are no doubt still consumers of music products and styles that they believe suit and express their particular belief systems. This may be why we have resurgences of music, styles, fashions and attitudes. Something that marketing men [sic] are not slow to package and sell, e.g. Britpop and Girlpower. *(p43)*

### Afterlife

The title had at least one reader outside the university. In 2011 Gol rewrote Billy Bragg's *Waiting For The Great Leap Forwards* for the year of Tahrir Square, WikiLeaks and the phone-hacking scandal. Where Bragg's original has *"Mixing pop and politics he asks me what the use is"*, Gol's version borrows from Ron:

> From folk protest to popular product, I wonder what my use is

### About this edition

This is a scan of Ron's original bound copy, presented as written in 1999. That includes the period terminology and the 1999 web addresses in the references, most of which no longer exist. The pictures and four appendices reproduce other people's copyright material (photographs, the lyrics of *Strange Fruit* and *God Save The Queen*, the Bill Grundy transcript and EMI's 1976 AGM statement), so they are left out of this web edition. A note at the front lists them. Everything else appears as submitted.

The original work speaks for itself. If it raises the obvious question of what Ron makes of it now, in the age of streaming, Suno and Hovercraft's return, that's for him to answer. One day, perhaps, he will.
