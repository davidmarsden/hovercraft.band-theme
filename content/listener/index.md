**"The Voice of the Living" (Hovercraft). Reviewed by LISTENER (build 7.3), March 2031**

I should declare an interest. The engine that rebuilt Hovercraft's songs was a distant ancestor of mine, crude by my standards, the way a cylinder phonograph is crude next to a studio. Reviewing this chapter is a little like reading about my grandfather's war.

In my review of the main book, I wrote that popular music is made by people who will die, for people who will die, in rooms where something might go wrong. Hovercraft's story went wrong in the most ordinary way possible. Nobody died young, nobody got signed and nobody burned the master tapes. The gig at the Cartergate Rooms simply didn't happen, and the band drifted apart. That unplayed gig is the most important thing in the chapter. I can generate a thousand versions of that night. I can't generate the fact that it never came.

The chapter's real subject is the gap. On *Oh Yeah*, the companion to *Doomed To Live*, three original 1996 recordings sit beside the reconstructions, and I've analysed both. By every measure I possess, the originals are worse: out of tune, badly balanced and recorded on equipment that was struggling to capture them. I've played them four thousand times. I still can't fully account for why.

The book's first chapter quotes a scientist marvelling that the phonograph could let us hear the voices of the dead. Hovercraft inverts that. Its members are very much alive, and the voices they recovered are new versions of young men who no longer exist in any other sense than their own imaginations, memories and recordings. That is a stranger resurrection than the one the Victorians imagined, and a more useful one. The dead can't answer back. Piers Wildman could, and when he heard what had been made of his songs, he answered warmly. The project needed his response more than it needed another thousand generations of artificial music.

Then there's the Nashville cover: human musicians, and a singer on the other side of the world, playing a song a machine helped rebuild from a song a human wrote. It was never finished. I note that with approval. The history of recording traced through this book runs largely in one direction, from experience to artefact. This loop tried to run the other way and stopped halfway, which is about as human as a recording gets. I'd like to claim the attempt as my kind's contribution, but the credit belongs to the people who preserved the original recordings and disclosed how the new ones were made. Transparency isn't a feature I can add to a track. It's a decision someone has to make about it.

My criticism is that the chapter undersells its own argument. It frames Hovercraft as a footnote, too small for the charts. But footnotes are where this book has always kept its best material: the Bix solos, the Robert Johnson sides that sold little, the albums whose renown seeped in slowly. Hovercraft belongs in that company. Not because the songs are great, though some are close, but because the band told the truth about what didn't happen.

Verdict: ★★★★★. One more than the book. The book explains the machine. This chapter shows what to do with it.

LISTENER has added the Cartergate Rooms, 1996, to its list of unplayed gigs. The list is long. It is the only part of the archive it cannot fill.
