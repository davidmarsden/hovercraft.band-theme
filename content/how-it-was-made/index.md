**The technology could transform what had been recorded. It could not recover what had never been played.**

Hovercraft's new recordings begin with the band's own. There are tapes from 1995 and 1996, imperfect but unmistakably human: guitars, drums, voices, and songs that existed long before generative AI, although the band had already used a machine, a Boss Dr Rhythm, to keep time.

Thirty years later, Gol began feeding those original recordings into an AI music engine, Suno, using the band's own performances as the foundation for new versions. It was not an automatic process. Perhaps nine out of ten early attempts were discarded straight away. The machine produced music, certainly, but often music that bore little resemblance to Hovercraft. Songs wandered off into unfamiliar genres, changed their melodies, misplaced their rhythms or acquired singers who sounded nothing like Charlie Pepper.

He learned to direct it: what kinds of source material worked, which instructions helped, what the machine could be persuaded to keep and what it stubbornly insisted on changing. Each time the technology improved, the instrument had to be learned again.

### The voice

One of the hardest problems was the voice. Piers Wildman's singing had a particular accent, phrasing and pronunciation, inseparable from the character of the songs. The AI could produce a convincing rock singer far more readily than *this* rock singer. Words, phrases, whole lines and sometimes entire songs had to be rewritten phonetically. That wasn't to change what Pepper had written, but to make the machine sing it as he had.

A technology capable of generating orchestras still needed a man from Grimsby to explain how a man from Winchester pronounced his words.

### Evidence and interpretation

The distinction matters. The original recordings are evidence. The new recordings are interpretations. They aren't restorations in the archival sense, and nobody pretends otherwise.

Nor was there a single definitive Hovercraft waiting to be rediscovered. Gol imagined what the band might have sounded like after a few more years of playing, with better equipment, more experience and a producer who understood what they were trying to do. Ron encouraged the whole enterprise, sometimes acting as taskmaster. But the recordings are largely one member's vision. Pepper might have imagined something entirely different. So might Ron or Mr Shimble.

*Doomed To Live* is not what Hovercraft sounded like. It is one version of what they might have become, imagined thirty years later by someone who was there. That's why *Oh Yeah* closes with three of the original 1996 recordings: so you can hear the gap between the document and the dream.

### What the machine can't do

The machine was trained on music made by countless other people. The band can account for its own source material, but not for everything the machine learned before it met these songs. There's no tidy resolution to that. What Hovercraft can do is say how the records were made, credit the people who wrote and played the songs, and keep the historical recordings separate from the new interpretations.

AI helped tell the story as well as rebuild the music, and that taught the same lesson. Asked why the band split, a machine supplied a neat explanation: jobs, distance, rehearsals gradually drying up. It was plausible, economical and wrong. The truth involved a departing singer, a house rented from a vicar, a European football championship and four young men whose ambitions didn't quite line up. Nothing could recover that without someone who remembered it.

The technology can widen the possibilities. It can't relieve anyone of the obligation to tell the truth.
